# H13 — Search a Population of Prompts
_parent: **Search a Population Against an Executable Fitness** (`population_search_executable_fitness`) · corpus support: 44 papers (accepted 15 · rejected 19 · unlabeled 10)_

## Tactical pattern
The genome is natural-language control text: a task instruction, a system prompt, a demonstration set, named prompt
components, or a whole prompt-like agent configuration. An LLM supplies the variation (paraphrase, phrase-level edits,
component mutation, LLM-executed crossover), a small scored evaluation set supplies fitness, and an allocation rule
(bandit, Bayesian surrogate, tournament or rating system, dueling comparisons) decides which candidates receive the
scarce evaluation budget. Direction comes from selection pressure, not from diagnosing individual errors. The decisive
design choice is what the fitness rewards — worst case over a shift or a paraphrase neighbourhood, an end-to-end
metric credited across the modules of a multi-stage pipeline, performance over a collection of tasks, or pairwise
preference when no scalar exists — far more than which metaheuristic drives the variation. Success is certified by
improvement on examples kept apart from the scoring set, at a stated evaluation budget.

## Step-by-Step
1. **Fix the genome and its neighbourhood.** Decide which text is mutable (whole prompt, components, demonstrations,
   system-level instruction, non-text parts of a multimodal prompt) and which edit operators define neighbours. Keep
   the edit vocabulary composable so it can be varied independently of the search strategy.
2. **Define fitness before searching.** Pick the score and the scoring set, and decide whether the target is average
   accuracy, robustness, multi-task coverage or preference. If no trustworthy scalar exists, pairwise judgements or
   debates can substitute, but guard against implicit criteria drifting away from the user's objective.
3. **Separate scoring from reporting.** Examples that drive selection must be disjoint from reported examples;
   sampling development examples from the reported test set, or tuning analysis choices on test examples, appears in
   member limitations and is the first thing a reader checks.
4. **Make budget explicit.** Allocate evaluations with a racing, bandit or surrogate rule and report optimizer cost in
   calls or tokens next to the gain. Single-seed baselines and ablations are a recorded weakness of rejected members.
5. **Show what the search adds.** Ablate against a simpler selector (greedy, random, best initial proposal) and a
   no-search baseline. A new swarm or hierarchy metaphor, or an ensemble of existing optimizers, with no new objective
   or genome is the shape most rejected high-fit members take; skipping this step is what sinks them.

## Differentiation within parent
- **vs H14:** H13 evolves text whose fitness is a model's scored behaviour on a small labeled set; H14 evolves
  executable programs judged by an automated evaluator that runs them.
- **vs H15:** some H13 members condition proposals on a score-sorted trajectory or a component memory, but that is
  raw history; H15 distils history into explicit, credited rules or skills.
- **vs H16:** H13 usually keeps top-k elites; H16 is defined by retaining a frontier or niches of complementary
  candidates. Critique-directed rewriting of prompts belongs to a different parent pattern.

## When to pick this one
The model is a black box, gradients are unavailable, a modest labeled scoring set exists, and the lever is wording,
instructions or demonstrations. The tell is that feedback arrives only as a score or a preference, so errors cannot be
individually diagnosed. If failures produce readable traces, a critique-directed rewrite fits better; if the artifact
runs against an automatic evaluator, use H14; if heterogeneous inputs need different prompts, use H16.

## Tactical failure mode
The root assumption that fails is that a small scoring set is representative of deployment and that surface edits
can supply what the task needs. Member limitations name the ceiling directly: edits cannot add substantially new task
information, search is sensitive to initialization, the optimizer does not exploit individual error cases, and tens of
labeled examples are still needed to limit overfitting. Rejected members fail in 2 recognisable ways. First, prompt
search is one tool inside a domain application (attack generation, bias elicitation, recommendation summaries,
persuasion games, federated tuning, image-prompt alignment): 13 of the 19 rejected members are low-fit. Second, a new
metaheuristic or ensemble is offered with no change to what fitness measures. Rejected records are also thin: 16 of 19
carry no recorded limitations, against 7 of 15 accepted.

## Lessons
### From accepted work
- Redefining fitness (worst case over a shift, robustness over meaning-preserving paraphrases, a shared system
  prompt scored across many tasks) is a stronger contribution than a new variation operator.
- Multi-stage pipelines can be tuned from an end-to-end metric alone by bootstrapping demonstrations from successful
  traces and assigning credit across modules.
- Decoupling the edit neighbourhood from the search strategy lets standard metaheuristics work under a small,
  reported evaluation budget.
- Treating the prompt as a distribution with a prior, or stabilising noisy online zeroth-order updates, replaces
  fragile point estimates.
- When text-only search plateaus, enlarging the genome (non-text prompt parts, whole agent configuration) can help.
### From rejected work
- A leader-follower swarm or an ensemble of existing optimizers, without a new objective, reads as a re-skin.
- Embedding prompt search as one component of a domain pipeline leaves the search contribution unclear.
- Reporting variance for the main method while baselines and ablations run at a single seed undercuts the comparison.
- Proxy fitness signals in adversarial applications invite doubt about what is actually being optimized.
- A record with no stated limitations gives reviewers nothing to calibrate the claim against.
