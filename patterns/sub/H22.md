# H22 — Co-Train a Proposer Against a Solver
_parent: **Self-Generate the Curriculum** (`self_generate_the_curriculum`) · corpus support: 30 papers (accepted 14 · rejected 5 · unlabeled 11)_

## Tactical pattern
Task generation becomes a learned policy in its own right. A proposer — often the solver's own weights in a second role, sometimes a separate model — is rewarded for producing tasks at the solver's current frontier (an intermediate success band, solver uncertainty, regret against a reference agent, or measured learning progress), while the solver is reinforced on those tasks with verifiable rewards (execution, generated tests, retrieval checks, preserved reference answers) or consensus rewards. The curriculum co-evolves with no external task data. The decisive design choice is not the difficulty reward but what keeps a difficulty-seeking proposer honest: an anchor of task validity and relevance that the proposer cannot game — an executable environment, a retrieval corpus, documents hidden from the solver, a solvability reference agent, unsolved real-data goals, or a fixed validation environment. Success is certified by solver gains on held-out tasks outside the generated distribution, against a static-synthetic or frozen-generator baseline, and by showing the gain survives several co-evolution rounds instead of plateauing or collapsing.

## Step-by-Step
1. **Pick a validity anchor before anything else.** Choose a domain where a generated task can be checked without trusting the proposer (code execution, runnable repositories, retrieval verification, answer-preserving transforms). If only consensus is available, treat it as noisy and guard it explicitly; five accepted members themselves list majority or consensus labels reinforcing shared errors as a limitation.
2. **Define the proposer's reward as a frontier signal, tied to that anchor.** Use a success band, uncertainty, regret, or learning progress measured on a fixed held-out validation environment. A proposer rewarded only for progress, difficulty, diversity or learnability, with no independent validity check, is the shape of 4 of the 5 rejected members; skipping the anchor is what sinks them.
3. **Filter, then decide role coupling and cost.** Gate proposals for solvability and quality before the solver trains; decide shared versus separate weights; budget the nested sampling cost explicitly, since difficulty estimation needs repeated solver rollouts per proposal (one accepted member's main contribution is making this affordable).
4. **Alternate updates and watch the dynamics.** Report the curve over co-evolution rounds and a stopping rule; accepted members themselves report eventual degradation, plateaus after a few iterations, and early saturation for small models.
5. **Certify outside the loop.** Evaluate on held-out and cross-task benchmarks against a fixed-data baseline, with seed variance, and disclose every seed set or warm-up dataset — a "data-free" claim that quietly rests on seed problems or external warm-up examples is easy for a reviewer to puncture, and one rejected member reports only a handful of iterations with no training-seed variance.

## Differentiation within parent
Here both roles are learned and coupled by a difficulty reward, so the curriculum is the output of a two-player optimization. H23 instead synthesizes tasks from diagnosed weaknesses or schedules with a generator that is prompted, procedural, or planned rather than co-trained as an adversary. H22 therefore inherits two-player problems (non-convergence, drift, collapse of the proposer into invalid or trivial tasks); H23 inherits diagnostic problems (noisy weakness signals, generator ceilings).

## When to pick this one
The tell: solutions are cheap to verify, but the task space is too open to hand-parameterize or to diagnose by rules, and the base model is already strong enough to propose valid tasks (members list "gains depend on initial model capability" and "weak solvers need sufficient capability to bootstrap valid tasks"). Prefer H23 when you can name and measure what the learner gets wrong, when a procedural generator with difficulty knobs already exists, or when the learner is too small to be a reliable proposer.

## Tactical failure mode
The root assumption that fails is that a proposer rewarded for difficulty will keep producing tasks that are both valid and relevant on its own. It breaks three ways:
- **Validity drift:** hardness rewards favour ambiguous or wrong tasks, and consensus or same-family judges ratify shared errors instead of catching them.
- **Relevance drift:** tasks become hard without becoming useful — one accepted member fixes this with unsolved real-data goals, while a rejected member that targets downstream gain validates only in maze environments.
- **Dynamic collapse:** repeated co-evolution degrades or plateaus.

In the rejected members the reward is typically specified as progress, difficulty, diversity or learnability with no stated validity check (4 of 5), evaluation is narrow, and 3 of 5 carry no extracted limitations at all.

Learning progress is not itself fatal: an accepted member uses it, but measures it on a fixed ground-truth validation environment.

## Lessons
### From accepted work
- Ground the solver's reward in something the proposer cannot fake: execution, generated tests, retrieval verification, a runnable environment, or answer-preserving variants of already-solved problems.
- Reward the proposer for a difficulty band or uncertainty and filter for solvability before training; the band is what keeps the curriculum learnable.
- If the proposer is rewarded for learning progress, measure progress on a fixed held-out validation environment, not on its own outputs.
- Condition generation on unsolved real-data goals to keep hard tasks pointed at capabilities that matter.
- Treat nested proposer-solver sampling cost as a first-class problem; making it affordable can be the contribution.
### From rejected work
- A proposer rewarded only for progress, difficulty or diversity, with no independent validity check, does not carry the case by itself.
- Claiming co-evolution from a trivial seed without external supervision invites the question of what keeps tasks valid — answer it up front.
- Validating a downstream-gain proposer in a single toy environment family leaves its generality unsupported.
- A few co-evolution rounds without seed variance cannot separate sustained improvement from noise.
