# H23 — Synthesize Tasks from Diagnosed Weaknesses
_parent: **Self-Generate the Curriculum** (`self_generate_the_curriculum`) · corpus support: 33 papers (accepted 16 · rejected 8 · unlabeled 9)_

## Tactical pattern
Instead of training an adversary, read the learner's current state and let it steer generation. The diagnostic is a measured signal from the current policy — failed episodes, high-perplexity or uncertain instances, forgetting and boundary signals, reward spread across sampled responses, frontier accuracy, the gap between independent and guided solving, or stagnation. A generator (prompted, procedural, a planner, or a bandit over task categories) then rewrites, simplifies, composes, re-difficulties or wraps tasks and environments — including procedural difficulty windows, environments and rewards written as code, and simulated experience grounded in replayed trajectories — and the results are verified and fed back into training, with re-diagnosis every round. The decisive choice is the pair (diagnostic signal, verifier): the diagnostic decides what to make, and an independent check certifies that what was made is valid and at the intended difficulty. Success is certified by held-out gains over static data or random and heuristic curricula, ideally against untargeted generation of the same volume.

## Step-by-Step
1. **Choose a real diagnostic, not a difficulty knob.** Pick a per-instance or per-skill signal measured on the current learner. Raising difficulty by a generic schedule — deeper composition, difficulty buckets, a solved-level floor — is the main mechanism of 3 of the 8 rejected members; it reads as a schedule, not a diagnosis.
2. **Choose the generation operator to fit the signal.** Rewrite or evolve failing items, recursively simplify unsolvable ones, compose verified atomic tasks into harder ones, wrap an environment's interface while keeping its logic and verifier, or program new environments and rewards. Keep an archive of solved and failed tasks as stepping stones.
3. **Verify every generated task independently of the generator.** Use an executable verifier, compiler feedback, a tool-necessity check, or fresh-rollout validation. Two rejected members rely on a learned reward model or a model-generated success judgment as the main certification; members using learned judges list misjudgment as a limitation.
4. **Close the loop and control drift.** Re-diagnose after each training round, constrain policy updates, and track forgetting alongside weakness; members report saturating task pools, catastrophic forgetting, and generated variants harder than their parents.
5. **Certify and cost it.** Report held-out and cross-task gains against untargeted synthesis of matched budget, the cost of repeated diagnosis and retraining, and a leakage guard whenever the diagnostic reads benchmark failures — only 1 of the 12 evidence-carrying members tested leakage.

## Differentiation within parent
H22 co-trains a proposer with a learned difficulty reward; here no adversary is trained. The generator acts on the learner's measured deficit through diagnostics or schedules, so H23 avoids two-player instability but is bounded by diagnostic quality and by the generator, which is often a strong fixed teacher model ("fixed scientist limits exploration", "synthesis depends on strong teacher models").

## When to pick this one
The tell: you can name and measure what the learner gets wrong, or the domain already has procedural generators or code-defined worlds with difficulty parameters, or the learner is too weak to be a reliable proposer and a stronger teacher is available. Prefer H22 when the task space is too open to diagnose by rule, the learner is strong enough to propose valid tasks, and verification is cheap enough to absorb nested proposer-solver sampling.

## Tactical failure mode
The root assumption that fails is that the diagnosed weakness is the thing worth training on and that the synthesized task actually exercises it. Members name the cracks:
- diagnostics are noisy while competence is low and need a sufficiently capable learner;
- synthesized variants drift in difficulty;
- synthetic data carries generator bias;
- fixed pools saturate;
- targeted data causes forgetting.

Among rejected members, the reward is either a generic difficulty schedule (3 of 8) or a learned proxy for preference or task success (2 of 8).

Near-twin mechanisms split by outcome: an accepted bandit learns which task categories help from a learning-gain proxy, while a rejected bandit schedules across difficulty buckets of rewritten problems; an accepted composer verifies tool necessity at each composed level, while a rejected composer only deepens dependency chains and rewards outcomes.

A trace-guided rejected generator shows the certification step can be the weak link: its curricula are rewarded by gradient alignment with the current solver rather than by an independent check of each generated task.

## Lessons
### From accepted work
- Seed generation from the learner's own failures or uncertain instances and regenerate every round instead of synthesizing once.
- Build harder tasks by composing verified atomic ones, so each level inherits a known solution and is checked again.
- Advance procedural difficulty only when frontier accuracy crosses a threshold, keeping reward variation informative.
- Generating whole environments and reward functions as code, from an archive of learned and failed tasks, escapes hand-parameterized task distributions.
- Track forgetting next to weakness and constrain updates, so targeted data does not erode earlier competence.
### From rejected work
- A generic difficulty schedule presented as self-generated curriculum reads as a knob, not a diagnosis.
- When the only certification is a learned reward model or a model-judged success signal, the curriculum inherits that proxy's errors.
- Feeding a small student's divergences back into synthesis is capped by the student's own capacity.
- Rewarding generated curricula by gradient alignment with the solver ties task quality to that signal; verify the tasks themselves.
