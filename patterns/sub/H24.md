# H24 — Evolve Rubrics as Training Rewards
_parent: **Co-Evolve the Evaluator** (`coevolve_the_evaluator`) · corpus support: 9 papers (accepted 3 · rejected 1 · unlabeled 5)_

## Tactical pattern
Stop treating the reward as a fixed specification and regenerate it from the policy's current outputs instead.
Per-query or per-task criteria (rubric items, reward/bonus terms, objective weights) are proposed from contrasts
among fresh rollouts. Each one is scored on how well it separates better responses from worse ones, and kept only if
it is discriminative and tracks correctness or some external grounding. The surviving criteria become dense reward,
step-level credit, or a trajectory filter for the next update. In several members a single shared policy also
learns to write the rubric. The decisive design choice is the **retention test**: what earns a criterion its place
(reward variance across samples, correlation with a verifiable outcome, peer consistency, a held-out validation gate,
evidence retrieved from outside the model). Success is certified by downstream policy gains on held-out
evaluations, not by the rubric reading well. The strongest members also report what the evolving rubric costs.

## Step-by-Step
1. Diagnose why the fixed reward fails: it is underspecified (outcome-only), stale (a frozen process reward model),
   hackable, or mis-weighted across objectives. Write down which of these the evolving rubric is meant to fix,
   because that choice determines the retention test.
2. Generate candidate criteria from contrasts in the current rollouts: positive and negative criteria per query,
   grounded where possible in evidence retrieved from outside the policy. Criteria that only restate the policy's
   own beliefs give the reward nothing new to say.
3. Filter criteria by discriminativeness and by agreement with something the policy cannot write (verified
   outcomes, retrieved evidence, a held-out validation gate). Closing the loop entirely inside one shared backbone,
   with task generator, solver and reward model co-trained and nothing checking them from outside, is what sinks the
   rejected member. Its own limitation says verification capacity caps improvement and late gains need real
   verification data.
4. Convert the retained criteria to reward at the granularity you need (trajectory score, objective weights,
   sign-preserving redistribution of advantage to cited steps). Keep a rubric memory or archive so criteria do not
   churn every step.
5. Evaluate on held-out tasks and report rubric-generation and judging cost next to the gains. Validate the rubric
   itself (human agreement, step-attribution correctness), not only the end-task score. Unlabeled members repeatedly
   list exactly these as unvalidated.

## Differentiation within parent
H24 uses the evolving evaluator as the reward that drives weight updates (RL or reward-filtered fine-tuning). Its
sibling H25 revises evaluators that serve as acceptance gates, benchmarks or measurement instruments for candidates
(skills, agents, prompts, models), and it anchors their validity explicitly. In H25 the evaluator's correctness is
the deliverable. In H24 it is instrumental, and it is judged by what the trained policy can do afterwards.

## When to pick this one
The tell: you are training a policy on open-ended or long-horizon outputs, and outcome-only reward is too sparse or
too coarse, a fixed process reward model goes stale as the policy improves, or fixed objective weights spend effort
on objectives that are already saturated. You also have a verifiable outcome or retrievable evidence for at least
part of the data, to anchor criterion retention.
Pick H25 instead when no weights are updated and you need a trustworthy grader to select or rank artifacts. H25 also
fits better when the only anchor is a tiny trusted label set that must certify the grader before anything is
optimized against it.

## Tactical failure mode
The root assumption that fails is that a reward model co-trained with the policy will keep supervision informative
as tasks get harder. Without an external anchor, generator, solver and judge share blind spots. Verification
capacity becomes the ceiling, and late-stage gains need the very real verification data the loop was meant to
replace. Secondary failure modes that members list against themselves:
- rubric generation and judging add rollout and training cost (one accepted member reports 27000 H100 GPU hours
  beyond supervised fine-tuning);
- criteria inherit judge biases and depend on proprietary models;
- test-time variants reuse the test prompts during adaptation;
- end-task gains are offered as if they validated step attribution, which was never checked;
- training-time variability goes uncharacterized;
- dynamic weighting relaxes standard clipping and KL safeguards to stay tractable.

of the retained rubric, or run-to-run variance.

## Lessons
### From accepted work
- Keep a criterion only if it creates reward variance across samples. Retaining criteria for discrimination stops
  the rubric from collapsing into a constant or a hackable target.
- Ground criteria in evidence the policy did not produce: paired positive and negative criteria built from
  retrieved evidence are load-bearing in the high-fit accepted member.
- The evaluator need not be prose. Adapting objective weights, or reward/bonus pairs, to measured progress during
  training is the same move, and it was accepted twice in low-fit form.
- Generate and filter reward candidates during policy improvement rather than training each candidate from
  scratch. That is what makes reward search affordable.
- State the evolving evaluator's compute bill and limitations openly, and evaluate on held-out sets.
### From rejected work
- A fully self-contained loop (task generator, solver and judge on one backbone) runs out of verification headroom.
  Design the external anchor in from the start instead of conceding late that real verification data is needed.
- Evaluating only one environment type leaves a self-learning loop's claim of generality unsupported.
- In the one rejected member, an improver comparison and released code did not make up for unclear held-out
  separation. Showing that the evolved reward generalizes beyond its own loop is the claim to make first.
