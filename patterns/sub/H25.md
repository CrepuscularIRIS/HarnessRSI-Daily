# H25 — Revise the Evaluator Against a Trusted Anchor
_parent: **Co-Evolve the Evaluator** (`coevolve_the_evaluator`) · corpus support: 16 papers (accepted 5 · rejected 4 · unlabeled 7)_

## Tactical pattern
The evaluator (verifier, test suite, detector set, rubric, benchmark) is a living artifact. It is repaired,
strengthened or replaced whenever it disagrees with a small trusted anchor: hidden oracle pass/fail bits, a sparse
labeled set, deterministic checks on cached executions, reproducible trajectories, or independently verifiable
external facts. The decisive design choice is **isolation**. The anchor has to be something the candidates under
evaluation can neither see nor write. An evaluator edit is accepted only if it strictly improves agreement with the
anchor, often backed by rollback, frozen examples, preserved criterion identities, or erasure of scores computed
under the evaluator it replaces. Success is certified first by evaluator validity (agreement with the anchor,
reproducibility, resistance to gameable mutations) and only then by what the gated selection produces.

## Step-by-Step
1. Name the trusted anchor and its budget (oracle bits, a few human labels, ground-truth checks, reproducible
   executions), and decide how often it may be consulted. Repeatedly consulting a hidden oracle, or a nominally
   locked test set, on the very tasks being evolved is a leakage path. Several members flag it in themselves.
2. Isolate the evaluator from the candidates. Options include a separate surrogate verifier, expectations formed
   before the candidate is seen, and fixed cached executions so that repairing the verifier cannot change task
   outcomes. A judge that sees the candidate first drifts toward agreeing with it.
3. Drive evaluator revisions from disagreements: surrogate pass vs anchor fail, judge vs reference probe, a
   regression on one dimension hidden inside an aggregate. Gate each edit on strict improvement against the anchor.
   Roll back drift, and erase scores that depended on a replaced evaluator.
4. Keep the anchor independent of the downstream score being optimized. Two of the four rejected members follow the
   same failing shape: calibrate the evaluator once, by correlation with a single small human-rated batch, then
   optimize against it with no refresh and no independent validity test.
5. Report evaluator validity separately from downstream gains (held-out tasks, human construct validation,
   variance), and state what adaptive evaluation costs in latency and compute.

## Differentiation within parent
H25 is about anchoring evaluator validity for selection, ranking, auditing and measurement. Its candidates are
skills, agents, prompts, benchmark tasks or safety tests, and usually no weights are updated against the evaluator.
Its sibling H24 uses an evolving rubric as dense RL reward and judges it by trained-policy gains. In H25 a
trustworthy evaluator is itself the contribution, and the trusted anchor is explicit.

## When to pick this one
The tell: the grader is the weak link. Fixed tests saturate, judges over-validate, or there is no executable metric
for the deployed task. On the other hand you hold a small trusted signal the candidates cannot game: opaque oracle
bits, sparse labels, reproducible execution, or external facts you can check. Use it when you must select or audit
artifacts, or keep a benchmark hard while it stays solvable.
Pick H24 instead when the evaluator's job is to supply dense reward for weight updates and you can judge validity
through held-out gains of the trained policy.

## Tactical failure mode
The root assumption that fails is that a small trusted signal, once used to calibrate the evaluator, goes on
certifying it. Rejected members either fit the evaluator to a small human-rated set (fidelity bounded by that set,
and every adaptation costs more human feedback), or make evaluation dynamic (inter-model competition plus prompt
optimization, adaptive hardening of safety tests) without naming an anchor that certifies the new measurement.
Members that pass review still list the next layer of the same problem:
- the anchor gets reused until it becomes a training signal (an oracle consulted repeatedly, selection run over and
  over on a small validation set, rollback that reads the locked test set);
- proxies are weak (one unlabeled member reports proxy-verifier agreement of only 60.7%);
- judges hallucinate or prefer their own outputs;
- two trust certificates share the same ensemble;
- validity holds only within an epoch;
- anchor benchmarks cap how far the evaluator can improve.

## Evidence profile
Only 3 of 16 members carry an `evidence` block, all of them accepted. No rejected member has one, so this
sub-pattern supports no evidence-based accepted-vs-rejected contrast.
- Held-out separation: `no` in 2/3, `unclear` in 1/3. Acceptance came without demonstrated held-out generalization.
- Retained-artifact ablation: `yes` in 2/3; `unclear` in 1/3.
- Variance: `yes` in 1/3. Optimizer cost: `partial` in 1/3. Code: `yes` in 1/3.
- Leakage guard: `untested` in 1/3, `unclear` in 2/3.
- Cross-task, budget matching, improver comparison: `unclear` in 3/3.
Typical report: ablation of the evolved evaluator component. Typical omission: held-out tasks and a leakage guard
on the anchor itself.

## Lessons
### From accepted work
- Pair an isolated surrogate verifier, which gives dense diagnostics, with an opaque oracle bit. Strengthen the
  tests only when surrogate success conflicts with the oracle, so the oracle never has to reveal answers.
- Form the expected behavior independently, before looking at the candidate, then judge conditioned on it. This
  directly targets a verifier's bias toward agreeing.
- Make each new evaluation item earn its place through reproducible execution trajectories. The benchmark then
  gets harder without becoming unsolvable.
- Turn judged failures into directives for the next round of targeted tests, and ablate the retained component
  (2 of the 3 evidence-bearing accepted members do).
- Gather requirement-level evidence from artifacts and traces instead of issuing one final verdict.
### From rejected work
- Calibrating an evaluator once, on a small human-rated batch, and then optimizing against it ties fidelity to that
  batch. Budget for refreshing the anchor and re-validating the evaluator.
- Choosing criteria or demonstrations by correlation with the same small labeled set used to score them is not an
  independent validity test.
- Making evaluation competitive or adaptive changes what is measured, but without a trusted anchor nothing
  certifies the new measurement.
- A near-identical mechanism was accepted in one framing and rejected in another. Do not over-read any single
  rejection in this sub-pattern.
