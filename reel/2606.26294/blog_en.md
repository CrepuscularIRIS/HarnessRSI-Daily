# The Red Queen Gödel Machine: when the evaluator evolves with the agent, what is actually shown?

Shen, Daniel Burkhardt, Meghdad Kurmanji, Nurbek Tastan, Lorenzo Sani, Niccolò Alberto Elia Venanzi, Jiayi Nie, Ambroise Odonnat, Zeyu Cao, Bill Marino, Xinchi Qiu, Rika Antonova, Nicholas D. Lane. The paper prints no public code link (it mentions a supplementary zip).*

## What the system changes about itself

The Red Queen Gödel Machine (RQGM) extends Huxley-Gödel-Machine-style archive search. Each archive node is a shared workspace holding task agents, **evaluators** (reviewers or graders written as editable prompts and code) and a meta-agent that edits the workspace. Only the scoring and orchestration harness is fixed. So the search edits not only the agents but also the learned evaluators that score them. The baseline, HGM-H, uses the same search with evaluators frozen.

## The loop, and the keep/reject rule

1. Search runs in **epochs**. Inside an epoch one evaluator per slot is frozen and scores every task agent, so the utility is stationary there. Node score is the 5th percentile (ε = 0.05) of a Beta posterior over its success rate.
2. At power-of-two checkpoints, every evaluator slot compares its incumbent with challengers on a **ground-truth anchor** (executable tests, human accept/reject decisions, human IMO grades). A challenger replaces the incumbent only if its anchor best-belief is higher; ties keep the incumbent. For a slot with an adversarial pool the compared score is a weighted objective (½ positive anchor items, ¼ negative anchor items, ¼ rejection of the pool; App. F.2.1 and F.6.3), so a challenger can win with lower anchor accuracy. The Fig. 9 caption says only anchor evidence enters the gate; App. F.2.1 adds the pool term.
3. On replacement, **selective erasure** drops every utility record that depended on the displaced evaluator; old nodes are re-scored on demand.
4. Optionally, outputs the displaced evaluator had accepted form an **adversarial pool**, and the next epoch also rewards rejecting them (used for paper reviewing and the DeepSWE reviewer).
5. The meta-agent sees the train split only; selection and replacement use validation only; the held-out test split is never seen during search. The coder and the evaluators have anchors and are chosen by global best-belief. The paper writer and prover have no anchor: they are chosen per epoch under that epoch's frozen evaluator, i.e. by the system's own evaluators, and then scored post hoc by external panels (Sec. 4, "Costs and reporting"). The meta-agent ran at high reasoning effort and the task agents at low effort in both DeepSWE arms.

## Headline results, as reported

- **DeepSWE** (GPT-6 Astra, search at low reasoning effort, 28 held-out tasks): at low effort the RQGM coder passes 82.1% against 75.0% for the HGM-H coder and 68.8% for the base GPT-6 Astra in mini-SWE-agent. At medium effort 85.7 / 78.6 / 76.8; at high effort 85.7 / 82.1 / 83.0 (Table 9).
- **Polyglot** (GPT-5.5 low, 166 held-out tasks): 71.7% for both the RQGM specialist and generalist against 69.9% for HGM-H, with 1.35×–1.72× fewer search tokens to exceed the baseline's pass rate (Table 8, Fig. 3).
- **Paper writing**, scored by a panel of four reviewers on 100 papers per writer: mean acceptance 21.8% (HGM-H writer) vs 38.8% (RQGM generalist, 1.78×) and 40.5% (RQGM specialist, 1.86×) (Table 2).
- **Proof writing** (20 IMO problems, three graders): panel mean score 4.33 for the RQGM specialist, 4.07 for the static IMO25 prover, 3.73 for HGM-H (Table 3).
- **Reward design** (quadruped; walking in search, jumping held out): 0.365 ±0.053 for the RQGM curriculum vs 0.306 ±0.128 for a static evaluator; 5/5 vs 3/5 runs produce a jump (Table 1).

## The controls that decide whether the gain is real

- **One run per arm.** The paper states "eight headline runs: an RQGM and an HGM-H run in each of the coding, paper, and proof domains"; that is six arms, or eight if Polyglot and DeepSWE count as two coding domains. No seeds, standard deviations or confidence intervals are given for the coding tables. The only multi-run result is reward design (five searches per arm).
- **Search cost.** On DeepSWE the winning RQGM coder was found after 587M search tokens, the HGM-H winner after 429M (Table 6), about 1.37‡× as many. The HGM-H run then continued to 2532.7M tokens (about 2104.1M‡ more) without finding a better coder, against 2876.8M for the full RQGM run (Table 14, 1.14‡×); the paper says the two searches reach similar best-belief at matched tokens. A held-out score at exactly matched tokens is not reported. In the paper domain the specialist writer used 221.8M tokens against 42.5M for HGM-H (5.2‡×); the 1.78× row (44.6M) is the token-matched one. The proof specialist used 88.0M against 21.5M (4.1‡×); the generalist (37.9M, 1.76‡× HGM-H's tokens, though the paper calls it matched) has exactly the same scores as HGM-H in Table 3.
- **Evaluator-side evidence is mixed.** On DeepSWE the final co-evolved reviewer had an anchor score of 0.48 (the first reviewers 0.03, then 0.12, 0.19, 0.22). The anchor is what the replacement rule selects on (with the pool term), so a rising anchor is expected by construction (Prop. 7, Remark 1). The paper's separate audit (Table 15) finds every reviewer seated before the last accepts 81.8%–100.0% of failing patches ("almost no signal"); only the final one separates them (accepting 20.8% of failing vs 35.0% of passing patches). On held-out APReS papers (Table 8) every RQGM reviewer is less accurate than the HGM-H reviewer: 84% (specialist and generalist) and 80% (adversarial) against 88%. The proof grader's advantage is stated in the text (Fig. 6, "3× lower search cost"); its values are only in the figure.
- **Attribution on DeepSWE.** The winning coder (found at 587M tokens) was generated after three of the 11 reviewer replacements, when the seated reviewer had anchor 0.22, one of the reviewers that Table 15 shows gives almost no signal. The abstract credits the co-evolved code-review signal; the winning-chain table (Table 19) shows the HGM-H chain also adopted the change "coder may add new regression tests" (its third node), which the RQGM winner includes. No coding-domain ablation separates the reviewer's contribution, so the attribution of the DeepSWE gain to the reviewer is not shown.
- **Mechanism ablations exist only for the paper and proof domains**, with Nemotron models rather than the headline models (Appendix D.2). In the paper domain a fixed reviewer saturates (writers reach 100.0% validation acceptance under it, scored by that same reviewer) and reviewer anchor accuracy is 78.2 ±4.4 against 95.0 ±10.3 with the full mechanism; dropping the adversarial pool (91.2 ±7.4) or erasure (92.9 ±10.8) stays inside the intervals. In the proof domain, the full mechanism's prover scores 3.60 [2.55, 4.67] against 3.05 [2.11, 4.04] for a coupled-HGM control, and its grader agrees with humans on 67.3% vs 65.7% (intervals overlap). No coding-domain ablation (reviewer off, replacement off) is reported.
- **Panels are partly the system's own evaluators.** The paper-writing panel includes the HGM-H reviewer and the RQGM adversarial reviewer. The proof panel includes the RQGM grader that co-evolved with the specialist prover: scores by that grader are 4.95 (specialist) vs 4.05 (IMO25). Without it, the specialist scores 4.03 against 4.08 for IMO25 and 3.60 for HGM-H (Table 10, Appendix D.1).
- **Splits.** Held-out sets are disjoint from search data (by task, paper or repository). The DeepSWE split (28/57/28 tasks) was built by the authors because the benchmark publishes none. The paper reports no contamination test of the base models against the benchmarks.
- **Cost accounting.** Blended tokens weight output at 5× input and count generation and evaluation calls. Validation evaluation carries 65%–70% of tokens. Reward design is compared to HyperAgents at "3.85–4.33× lower cost", but with a different model (Nemotron 3 Ultra vs Claude Sonnet 4.5) and list prices.

## Where the gain is absent or small

- **DeepSWE, high effort.** 85.7% vs 83.0% for base GPT-6 Astra and 82.1% for HGM-H: one task‡ of 28 separates the RQGM from HGM-H. At low effort the gap to HGM-H is two tasks‡ (23 vs 21 of 28), 7.1‡ points. These task counts assume one attempt per task for the evolved coders; Astra's figures are means over four attempts and the paper does not state repeated attempts for the evolved coders.
- **Polyglot.** 71.7% vs 69.9% is three tasks‡ of 166, with no interval; the gain claimed is mainly in tokens.
- **Paper writing, Sakana reviewer.** Acceptance 1.0 / 2.0 / 5.0% for the three writers, within the stated ±2.2 to ±4.3 intervals. The 1.86× specialist is not at matched tokens. The authors attribute the gain to the regularized search; no writer-panel ablation is reported.
- **Proof writing.** 4.33 ±0.41 vs 4.07 ±0.42 for IMO25 is within one standard error; on Pass@7 the specialist (48.3%) trails IMO25 (55.0%). The paper says the higher mean comes from more near-complete (6/7) proofs.
- **Adversarial reviewer.** It accepts 32% of held-out human papers (Table 8) and 29–31% of papers from the RQGM writers of the same run (Table 2), the paper's sign of calibration; it is less accurate than the HGM-H reviewer (80% vs 88%), a cost the paper notes. The HGM-H reviewer accepts AI-generated papers at 1.42×–1.91× the human rate.
- **Reward design.** 0.059‡ higher mean, smaller than the static arm's 0.128 standard deviation (pooled over 30 trials from five runs); the curriculum arm used 53.8M tokens vs 48.3M. The obstacle curriculum is authored by the researchers, not evolved.
- **Stated limits.** Stationarity inside an epoch is assumed; panels are automated, not human; the anchor bounds what evaluator evolution can reach.

## Our read

HarnessRSI score **70**, band ★★★, evidence level **improver**. The paper changes the improvement mechanism itself (evaluator replacement, adversarial pool, erasure) and compares it with a fixed-evaluator search on held-out data. It reports held-out splits, transfer of DeepSWE harnesses across reasoning efforts, token accounting and, in two domains, mechanism ablations. It does not report repeated runs or variance for the headline arms, a held-out score at exactly matched tokens for the DeepSWE winners (full-run totals are 2876.8M vs 2532.7M), ablations in the coding domain, or a contamination test. Most headline gaps are a few tasks or lie inside the reported intervals. The clearest evidence is the proof grader (text claim), the final DeepSWE reviewer's separation of passing from failing patches, and the large gaps on the paper-writing panel, which itself includes the system's own reviewers.
