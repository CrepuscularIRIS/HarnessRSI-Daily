arXiv: 2611.21847    title: Inventa: Decentralized Self-Improving Agents for Autonomous Scientific Discovery

[abstract]
Large language model (LLM) agents can generate research ideas and improve scientific discovery through iterative experimentation. However, increasing the number of agents or iterations does not automatically translate to producing more diverse or feasible ideas. Agents may converge on similar candidates, propagate unsupported claims through shared memory components and repeatedly apply search strategies that no longer yield any improvements. We introduce Inventa, a framework for automated scientific discovery that employs autonomous research units and verified shared knowledge. Each unit creates a lineage of candidate ideas using a mutator agent. A local orchestrator agent periodically adapts the mutator's harness. When progress stalls, the orchestrator agent replaces its model or restarts the idea evolution process. Verifiers evaluate each candidate idea and record verified improvements, useful components and constraint violations in a shared knowledge, the only channel between units. We evaluate Inventa on five benchmarks spanning the research cycle, covering retrieval (ScholarCatalyst), ideation (IdeaBench), proposal writing (IdeaScientist), and scientific law discovery (EurekaBench, SciLaws-Bench). It leads all five, achieving 73.6% on EurekaBench versus 56.2% for CORAL. On IdeaBench, it discovers 26.0 novel ideas per paper, compared with 18.6 for ResearchAgent, with 82% fewer model calls.

[intro/head from cached fulltext]
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to assist in scientific research, ranging from identifying relevant literature to proposing candidate hypotheses and implementing experiments. Evolutionary search extends these capabilities by using evaluation feedback to refine candidates. Systems such as AlphaEvolve ( Novikov et al., 2025 ) , EvoX ( Liu et al., 2026b ) , CORAL ( Qu et al., 2026 ) , and MILO ( Jana et al., 2026b ) show the potential of combining LLMs with closed-loop search. These advances motivate automated research assistants that can explore novel ideas, evaluate their feasibility and retain useful findings across experiments. Automated research assistance involves several related tasks across the complete scientific research cycle. These include retrieving prior work that provides inspiration for a research question ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , generating ideas from existing papers ( Guo et al., 2025 ) , assessing their novelty against related work ( Schopf & Färber, 2026 ) , and discovering scientific laws from data ( Huang et al., 2026 ) . They differ in output but share a common challenge: generating promising candidates is insufficient unless a system can also verify them and use the evidence to guide further exploration. Recent AI co-scientists , such as the Agentic Scientist ( Lange et al., 2026a ) and InternAgent-1.5 ( Feng et al., 2026 ) , coordinate multiple agents to generate, test, and refine hypotheses. Despite this progress, several challenges remain unresolved. First , maintaining diversity as search grows . These systems largely follow myopic hill-climbing to optimize the most promising candidates, which narrows the exploration radius ( Lange et al., 2026a ) . Furthermore, additional samples or iterations yield increasingly similar candidates, so extra computation brings diminishing returns ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Si et al., 2025 ) . Second , sharing useful knowledge without propagating errors . Isolated agents cannot benefit from one another’s findings, whereas interacting agents often converge on similar ideas ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . When shared information is not independently verified, unsupported claims also influence subsequent attempts ( Kim et al., 2025 ) . Third , adapting the proposal mechanism . Refining candidate ideas does not, by itself, change the prompts, tools, or control flow used to generate them, so iterations can improve ideas but not expand the search space. Fourth , satisfying scientific constraints . Highly novel ideas often prove infeasible under the task’s assumptions or physical requirements, creating a novelty–feasibility gap ( Geng et al., 2026 ) . To address these challenges, we propose Inventa (Fig. ), a framework that combines i) autonomous research units, ii) self-adaptive proposal mechanisms and iii) sharing of verified findings. We draw inspiration from how science advances in practice: research groups or labs pursue different approaches in a decentralized fashion while building on evidence reported by others. Each unit (like a lab) maintains its own idea lineage and uses a mutator agent (junior researcher) to develop new candidates. A per-unit orchestrator (the lab’s principal investigator, or PI) adapts the mutator’s harness and intervenes when progress stalls. Verifiers evaluate the candidates, return structured feedback, and add verified findings to the shared knowledge. The proposed framework separates three operations: developing candidate ideas, self-adapting the process that develops them, and determining which findings can be shared. This separation allows units to pursue different approaches while receiving, only through shared knowledge, common evidence about successful candidates, useful components, and observed constraint violations. \papersans Contributions. Our main contributions are: ❶ Self-improving idea generation. We propose Inventa , an agentic framework for scaling scientific discovery and research ideation. The framework refines ideas while continually evolving the agents that generate them, expanding the space of ideas it can reach as the search proceeds. ❷ Diverse exploration with verified knowledge sharing. We introduce autonomous research units that explore different approaches, each with its own history of ideas and experiments. Units communicate only through shared knowledge written by verifiers: positive findings point to promising directions, while refuted ones, such as constraint violations, prune the search space. ❸ Extensive experiments.
