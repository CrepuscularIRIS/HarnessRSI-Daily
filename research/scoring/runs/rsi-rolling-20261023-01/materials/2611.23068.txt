arXiv: 2611.23068    title: The Resolution Hypothesis: Agent Evolution's Key Is Identifying Improvements

[abstract]
Agent evolution follows a simple loop: a proposer writes candidate artifacts (prompts, skills, memories) and selection accepts those that score higher. This loop has shown promise, yet practitioners face a puzzle: methods trade wins across papers, and evolution often fails to bootstrap on certain tasks. We trace this puzzle to the Resolution Hypothesis: agent evolution stalls not because methods fail to propose better artifacts, but because noisy evaluations cannot reliably identify them. Across seven evolution methods and six models (over 10M LLM calls), we find support for the hypothesis at both levels where evaluation scores are used. At the candidate level (choosing artifacts), helpful candidate artifacts exist, but selection is no better than random at telling them from harmful ones, so errors accumulate across rounds. At the method level (ranking methods), typical margins of a few percentage points are smaller than the difference between two independent runs of the same method, so published rankings often rest on luck. Together, these findings indicate that agent evolution is often bottlenecked by reliably identifying improvements. This calls for a shift in how we develop and evaluate self-improving agents, from designing ever more candidate improvement systems to making true improvements detectable and accumulable.

Agent evolution improves frozen LLM agents by refining persistent external artifacts such as prompts, skills, memories, and entire agent harnesses. It typically follows a loop as shown in Figure 1 b: a proposer writes candidate artifacts, the resulting agent is evaluated on a task set, and selection accepts the candidate artifacts that score higher. Evolution methods of this family have shown promise across benchmarks and models [ 37 , 27 , 24 ] , making the prospect of systems that continuously improve their own workflows increasingly plausible. Yet the picture is far from settled. Studies often reach conflicting conclusions: the same methods flip between effective and ineffective across benchmarks, and their rankings trade places across papers. On Terminal-Bench [ 22 ] and other coding-agent benchmarks, evolution methods have repeatedly been reported to stall [ 29 , 17 , 7 ] , while on other benchmarks each new method claims to outperform its predecessors [ 43 , 18 , 24 , 31 ] . Whether evolution helps depends on the setting in ways the community cannot yet predict. This instability erodes confidence in this direction in both academia and industry, and rewards whichever method happened to be luckier at evaluation time. Yet unreliable evaluation affects more than the reported rankings of evolution methods. At the candidate level, the same kind of evaluation decides which artifacts to carry forward to the next round. As a result, the evolution methods themselves become unreliable. We argue that this evaluation rests on an untested premise that its scores can identify the improvements evolution brings. We find that the premise fails at both levels where the scores are used. At the method level, the margins that separate methods in papers are smaller than the difference between two independent runs of the same method: on Terminal-Bench, current common practice (Appendix D.3 ) can only identify the truly best of four text-artifact methods 33% of the time, barely above a random guess (Section 4 ). At the candidate level, the selector picks the better of two candidate artifacts only about half the time, no better than a coin flip (Section 5.2 ). Because each round accepts the artifact that happened to score highest, the scores go up but the artifacts do not get better (Section 5.3 ). Both failures trace to a single constraint, which we state as a hypothesis. The Resolution Hypothesis. The bottleneck of agent evolution is identifying which artifacts bring improvement, not proposing them. Why is identifying improvements so hard? Because model weights are frozen, an artifact helps only by changing the model’s behavior at inference time, while LLM trajectories also vary each time the agent attempts a task. As a result, separating a true behavioral change from evaluation noise is difficult. We define the resolution of an evaluation pipeline as the smallest true improvement it can reliably distinguish from noise. Once the true improvement falls below this resolution, candidate selection degrades to chance, so little improvement accumulates during evolution (Figure 1 a). Proposal determines how large the improvements on offer are; resolution determines how small an improvement can be and still be reliably identified. We test this hypothesis at a much larger scale than current practice. Whereas prior work typically relies on a single evolution run (Appendix D.3 ), we run each method 8 times per model and benchmark and evaluate each resulting artifact up to 16 times, totaling 1,030 independent evolution runs and over 2.1 million test trajectories. In total, the study covers seven agent evolution methods over six models and four agent benchmarks, plus nine further benchmarks on which we re-examine previously reported gains; to our knowledge, it is the largest reproducibility study of agent evolution to date. Our findings are: • Method level (Section 4 ). Typical method margins are smaller than what a single run can identify. The large historically reported gains are the clearest instance of our hypothesis: on those benchmarks, evolution succeeds by exploiting evaluation shortcuts that lift nearly every task at once, producing gains large enough to exceed the resolution of the evaluation, and stalls once the evaluation is repaired (Section 4.2 ). • Candidate level (Section 5 ). We derive three predictions from the hypothesis. All three are supported by our experiments on Terminal-Bench: P1 Candidate pools contain artifacts that genuinely improve task performance: proposal is not the bottleneck (Section 5.1 ). P2 Common selection mechanisms—whether based on execution scores or LLM judgments—perform near chance at identifying the better of two candidate artifacts (Section 5.2 ). P3 Per-round selection errors compound across rounds, producing the large differences between independent runs of the same method that flip rankings (Section 5.3 ).
