arXiv: 2611.23476    title: GUARD: Stabilizing Recursive Self-Improvement with Regret Guidance

[abstract]
Autonomous AI agents pair a frozen base model with an editable harness of prompts, skills, tool rules, and memory. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) adapts this harness from experience without weight updates, yet self-editing without external graders often backfires: reflection and scalar scores conflate what an edit newly broke with what remains unsolved, causing local regressions, skill forgetting, and memory bloat. We introduce GUARD (Grader-free Unsupervised Environment Design [UED] with Adaptive Rule Distillation), a closed-loop framework for reference-free continual harness evolution. Our key insight is to decompose each task into pre-execution self-checks and guide harness updates via directional regret. Each round grades trajectories against these checks, pinning mastered rules while prioritizing newly broken behaviors, and selects among parallel candidate edits by critic-free group-relative fitness. A Soft-HURT gate then promotes an edit only when gains outweigh regressions, scoping specialized fixes to prevent over-triggering and guaranteeing non-regression on its gated buffer. At test time, one frozen harness runs in a single pass. Across benchmarks on memory, skill evolution, continual task streams, and domain reasoning, GUARD consistently improves performance without catastrophic forgetting or memory pollution while substantially cutting deployment token usage.

[intro/head from cached fulltext]
Modern language model agents combine a frozen base model with a surrounding harness of prompts, reusable skills, tool interfaces, memory, and control logic [ 31 , 42 , 20 , 45 ] . In practice, this harness decides whether the same model checks the right state before acting, calls tools with valid arguments, recovers from failed commands, and carries useful rules across sessions [ 52 , 40 ] . Much recent progress in deployed agents comes from harness engineering , yet tuning harnesses by hand scales poorly: engineers can inspect only a small slice of failed runs, and fixed designs quickly go stale as tools, schemas, and user workflows change [ 5 , 49 ] . Recursive self-improvement (RSI) aims to automate this loop by turning past experience into persistent edits that improve future runs [ 54 , 38 , 44 , 26 ] . Because frontier models often sit behind black-box APIs with frozen weights and open-world tasks rarely provide ground-truth rewards for weight-space reinforcement learning [ 60 , 62 , 13 , 46 ] , treating the editable harness as a non-parametric policy around the frozen model offers a practical, inspectable way to improve agents after deployment [ 23 , 36 ] . Early automated methods search over prompts or agent code offline using labeled training splits before deployment [ 18 , 12 , 55 , 58 , 20 ] . The resulting static harnesses struggle when test-time tasks shift. Newer approaches regularize offline search for out-of-distribution generalization [ 45 ] , probe per-task harnesses online while saving reusable lessons [ 49 ] , or append reflections and skills across episodes [ 59 , 30 , 50 , 1 ] . Yet continually updating a single persistent harness across rounds without reference answers or external graders often degrades performance over time [ 5 ] . Because every edit changes subsequent rollouts, free-form reflection and scalar task scores mix two distinct failure signals: behaviors the latest edit newly broke and capability gaps the agent still has not solved . Mixing these signals across rounds causes three failure modes: local overfitting ( over-triggering ), where a broad rule written for recent failures breaks tasks that already worked; catastrophic forgetting , where shifts in workflow, topic, or domain overwrite earlier skills [ 5 ] ; and memory pollution , where unverified additions accumulate stale or conflicting rules that bloat the prompt. Over multiple rounds, self-editing can leave an agent worse than its starting point (Figure 1 ). Central Question. How can an autonomous agent continually improve its persistent harness in a reference-free setting without interfering with or catastrophically forgetting previously learned skills? We address this challenge by casting harness adaptation around a frozen model as gradient-free policy optimization in text space, guided by two ideas from reinforcement learning: curriculum regret from unsupervised environment design (UED) [ 7 , 14 , 15 , 35 ] and critic-free group-relative scoring from Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 37 , 3 ] . We never update the model weights or train a value critic: the harness is the only part of the policy that changes (§ 2 ). We implement this approach in GUARD ( Grader-free UED with Adaptive Rule Distillation ; Figure 2 , § 4 ). Before executing a task, GUARD compiles the task prompt and tool schema into a fixed checklist of atomic binary checks (§ 4.1 ) and grades completed runs with a two-layer (sandbox execution plus semantic trace) self-reviewer, because checking one requirement on a finished trace is simpler than satisfying every constraint during generation. Across rounds, Analyze (§ 4.2 ) pins criteria that stay satisfied and ranks newly broken criteria ahead of unsolved gaps; Propose (§ 4.3 ) samples a parallel group of candidate edits, scores them against each other on the same tasks, and contrasts the best and worst candidates to rewrite its proposal prompts; and Gate (§ 4.4 ) promotes the winner only when its gains exceed induced regressions ( Soft-HURT ), narrowing rules that cause isolated drops with [iff] preconditions and logging rejected edits. At test time, candidate pooling, self-review, and gating are turned off, and the agent runs a single pass ( pass@1 ) with its frozen harness. Three choices separate GUARD from prior harness learners. Reflexion and GEPA update from free-form reflection or scalar and Pareto scores, so they cannot tell a new regression from an old gap; GUARD ranks checklist criteria by paired directional regret. Meta-Harness searches agent code against raw trace archives, and RRSI rewrites one candidate per round behind a score-band gate that leaves per-task drops unbounded; GUARD scores a parallel group of candidates on the same tasks and promotes the winner only when its gains exceed induced regressions, narrowing rules with [iff] preconditions instead of deleting them.
