arXiv: 2611.24302    title: OpenKAGE: Open-Ended Lifelong Learning in the Open World via Knowledge-Sharing Agent-Group Evolution

[abstract]
Building autonomous agents that continually explore open worlds and acquire new capabilities through experience is a long-standing aspiration in artificial intelligence. Existing research often pursues lifelong learning within predefined tasks, curricula, or objectives, with self-improvement largely confined to individual agents. We introduce OpenKAGE, an agent-group evolution framework for open-ended lifelong learning. Within this framework, agents autonomously explore open worlds and continually acquire new capabilities by accumulating and reusing knowledge from their own interactions and the experience of others. OpenKAGE builds on two key mechanisms: (1) a two-layer knowledge representation, with a memory layer for situated memories and an experience layer for transferable experience and skills that retains the applicability conditions needed for reuse; and (2) a knowledge-sharing mechanism that semantically consolidates textual experience into a continuously evolving shared knowledge graph, while executable skills are shared verbatim. In Minecraft, OpenKAGE supports open-ended lifelong learning, enabling agent groups to sustain exploration and reach advanced technological milestones. Furthermore, the accumulated knowledge can be transferred to a newly initialized agent group on a different backbone model, improving its survival and exploration progress.

[intro/head from cached fulltext]
We study open-ended lifelong learning as a continuing process in which agents autonomously generate goals, accumulate knowledge from interaction, and reuse that knowledge to acquire new capabilities. Unlike learning toward a fixed terminal task, this process is not organized around a predefined endpoint. A suggestive image comes from The Wild Robot : cast ashore on an unfamiliar island with only her built-in directives, Roz learns to coexist with the wildlife and, by raising an orphaned gosling, comes to belong to the island. Future autonomous agents may likewise enter physical or digital worlds carrying only system-level directives, and must confront unfamiliar phenomena, discover new things to do, and devise new strategies without human intervention. Toward this vision, substantial progress has been made in enabling agents to learn and act in open worlds. Reinforcement and imitation learning support long-horizon control ( Baker et al., 2022 ; Hafner et al., 2023 ) , while LLM-based agents combine planning, memory, and reflection to tackle increasingly complex tasks ( Zhu et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ) . Systems such as Voyager, Optimus-1, and AriGraph further demonstrate how reusable skills, structured experience, and knowledge graphs can support knowledge accumulation and reuse ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Anokhin et al., 2025 ) . Despite these advances, much of the research remains organized around predefined tasks, curricula, or objectives, with self-improvement largely confined to individual agents. Although systems such as Voyager support autonomous exploration, an individual agent still learns primarily from its own interactions, limiting the range of experience available to guide its development. Open-endedness research does generate new challenges rather than fixing them, by co-evolving environments with their solvers or by asking a foundation model which tasks are interesting ( Wang et al., 2019 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ) , but this concerns what a single learner pursues next. Knowledge acquired by one agent does not automatically become available to others. This motivates moving beyond individual learning toward agent-group evolution, where experience acquired through diverse interactions can contribute to a shared, growing body of knowledge. Learning from others offers a natural route from individual learning to collective improvement. CAMEL and MetaGPT enable agents to collaborate through role-based communication and structured workflows ( Li et al., 2023 ; Hong et al., 2024 ) , while Generative Agents combines memory, reflection, and social interaction to support information diffusion and coordination ( Park et al., 2023 ) . More directly, Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) shares evolutionary traces across agents and turns them into framework-level improvements through reflection ( Weng et al., 2026 ) . Memory systems such as G-Memory and LLMA-Mem keep shared memory across LLM agents ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) , and selective-sharing methods pass only chosen experiences or policy modules between agents ( Gerstgrasser et al., 2023 ; Nath et al., 2026 ) . These approaches demonstrate different ways for agents to benefit from one another, from coordinating actions to improving their own implementations or sharing memory and experience, though most are evaluated with tasks given in advance. For open-ended lifelong learning, we focus on a complementary challenge: how to turn knowledge acquired through individual interactions into a shared resource that supports continued exploration and capability acquisition across the group. This calls for distinguishing memories tied to an agent’s particular circumstances from experience that can be reused by others, and continually consolidating the latter as agents explore and learn. To this end, we introduce OpenKAGE, an agent-group evolution framework for open-ended lifelong learning (Figure 1 ). OpenKAGE enables agents to autonomously explore open worlds and continually acquire capabilities by accumulating and reusing knowledge from their own interactions and the experience of others. Two mechanisms underpin this process. First, a two-layer knowledge representation separates private situated memories from transferable experience and skills, retaining the applicability conditions needed for reuse across agents. Second, a knowledge-sharing mechanism semantically consolidates textual experience into a continuously evolving shared knowledge graph, while executable skills are shared verbatim and remain maintained by their authors. Situated memories remain private, while transferable knowledge becomes a cumulative resource for the group rather than remaining isolated within individual agents. To bring this accumulated knowledge into subsequent decisions, intent-aware subgraph retrieval draws relevant knowledge from both the agent’s local graph and the shared graph to support its ongoing exploration.
