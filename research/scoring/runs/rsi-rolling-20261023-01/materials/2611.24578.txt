arXiv: 2611.24578    title: BaseRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement of a Base Vision-Language Model via Training-Free Harness Internalization

[abstract]
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is moving from the harness of prompts and tools around a base model toward the model itself. However, existing RSI still faces key challenges, including harness-bound gains that stay outside the base model, black-box supervision that reads only the model's outputs, and imprecise edits whose site and strength are fixed in advance. To address these challenges, we propose BaseRSI, a new paradigm of Recursive Harness Internalization: the base model improves its harness, then writes what the harness adds into its own weights through training-free edits, round after round on its own responses. To support this paradigm, we introduce ReGap, a mechanism that supervises every transformer block with the high-dimensional activation difference that the harness makes at that block alone, and updates the block's weights along this signal in a low-dimensional subspace. Building on this mechanism, we further propose ReKey, an algorithm that writes this signal by regression into a few key-value neurons of every block, and measures the site and strength of each edit on the model's own responses. Moreover, the base model fills every role of this recursion with forward passes alone, and its edits, written at different sites, compose in one checkpoint without refitting. Experimental results on ten visual reasoning benchmarks show that BaseRSI consistently improves a base vision-language model both in distribution and on held-out benchmarks. Our code is available at https://github.com/beita6969/BaseRSI.

In recent years, a variety of recursive self-improvement (RSI) systems have let foundation models improve the prompts, tools, and context they work with [ 32 , 31 , 82 ] , gradually moving from the harness around a frozen model toward improving the base model itself. In this process, the harness has become a key bridge from a frozen base model to a stronger system: by adding verified procedures and finer views to the request, a vision-language model can answer visual questions with improved accuracy, controllability, and reuse (Figure 1 ). However, in practice, these gains still live in the request rather than in the model [ 60 , 75 ] , making them costly on every request and leaving the base model unable to build on what its harness learns. Along this path from the harness to the model, three main lines of work have emerged, as shown in Figure 2 . First, harness evolution rewrites the prompts, tools, and programs around frozen weights [ 32 , 31 , 68 , 70 ] , treating the harness as the object of self-improvement. Second, model–harness co-evolution updates the weights together with the harness through fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or distillation [ 76 , 38 , 18 ] , moving harness gains into the model. Third, weight editing writes new behavior into the weights directly, as memories of facts and mistakes [ 44 , 45 , 26 ] or as updates computed directly from forward passes [ 46 , 72 ] . However, these methods still face three challenges (Figure 2 a–c). (i) Harness-bound gains. Harness evolution improves what surrounds a frozen model [ 32 , 31 , 68 ] ; every addition costs context again on each request and is lost when the harness is taken away [ 60 , 75 , 18 ] , so the gains accumulate outside the base model and its own capability stays where it started. (ii) Black-box supervision. Co-evolution and harness distillation supervise the weights only at the output of the model, with trajectories, rewards, or output distributions [ 75 , 58 , 38 , 81 ] ; what the harness changes inside the model stays hidden, so the signal travels back through every block by gradient training, which is slow and internalizes noise [ 18 ] . (iii) Imprecise edits. Weight editing sets the site and strength of an edit by a preset rule, in a few layers [ 45 ] or the last layer [ 26 ] ; localization does not tell where an edit works best [ 23 , 48 ] , so edits miss part of their target [ 6 ] and harm general abilities [ 21 ] . To address these challenges, we propose BaseRSI (Figure 2 d), a new paradigm of Recursive Harness Internalization that runs in two loops: in the outer loop, the base model adds candidate entries to its harness, and an acceptance test keeps those whose gain generalizes. In the inner loop, the base model with an accepted entry in the request is the teacher, the same model on the original request is the student, and the teacher and the unedited student first answer the development questions. To support this paradigm, we introduce ReGap , a mechanism that replays each response through the teacher and the student, takes the difference that the entry makes at every block alone, the teacher–student gap, as a high-dimensional target inside the model rather than at its output, and turns each gap into a low-dimensional update of that block’s weights. Building on this mechanism, we further propose ReKey , an algorithm that first measures on the student’s responses which weights the entry should be written into, then writes the gaps by regression into a few key–value neurons of every block, and finally measures the strength of the edit on held-out responses. The edited student then answers again, and since the edit has changed its responses, the next round repeats these steps on the new ones. After the last round, the edits written at different sites add up in one checkpoint, which answers the original requests alone. Throughout, the base model fills every role with its own forward passes, and BaseRSI joins harness evolution, weight editing, and internalization into one recursive loop that makes the base model itself stronger. We evaluate on five in-distribution and five held-out visual reasoning benchmarks across perception, counting, charts, spatial reasoning, and visual logic. Results show that BaseRSI outperforms prompting, visual zoom-in, harness evolution, and fine-tuning on every benchmark and carries over to other base models (RQ1, RQ2), that the edited checkpoint exceeds its own harness in the request while answering original requests on standard inference engines (RQ3), and that supervision inside the model and a measured site and strength each add to the gain (RQ4, RQ5). These results mark a step toward base models that recursively improve their own weights.
