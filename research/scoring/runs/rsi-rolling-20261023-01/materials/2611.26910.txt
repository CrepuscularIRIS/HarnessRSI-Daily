arXiv: 2611.26910    title: AstronautBench: Measuring Agent Self-Improvement Across Episodes Through Knowledge, Evidence, and Progress

[abstract]
LLM agents are expected to improve themselves from their own experience across episodes, with fixed weights. Self-improvement in an embodied world cannot be read from one measure. Existing benchmarks measure task progress, world-knowledge question answering (QA), and episodic memory, but a correct answer does not show that the agent ever observed the evidence for it, and progress does not show what the agent learned. We introduce AstronautBench, which measures one agent's self-improvement through three views on the same run: what it knows, whether its own evidence supports that knowledge, and how far it gets in the world. It is built on the game Outer Wilds, where each episode is a 22-minute loop after which the world resets, only the agent's knowledge carries over, and progress is gated by knowledge. After every episode, we measure the places reached and ship-log rumor facts revealed, the correctness of answers to 164 questions about the world, and whether each answer is supported by the frames and position the agent cites from its own recorded play. Two LLM judges from different model families check every answer. In eight-episode baselines with GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon planners, which act through a learned controller or their own gamepad inputs, the three views do not move together, so any one of them alone gives an incomplete picture of whether the agent is improving. The GPT-6.1 Sol agent's correct answers grow from 14.6% to 26.8% of all questions, but at episode 8 only 59 to 68% of them are supported by its cited evidence. It reveals no new rumor facts after episode 3, while the places it reaches and its memory keep growing. More than half of the answers that become correct after episode 3 cite only evidence from earlier episodes.

LLM agents are increasingly expected to improve themselves from their own experience across episodes ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) . By self-improvement we mean this kind of improvement, with fixed weights, close to what AgentOdyssey calls test-time continual learning, not a model training on its own outputs. An agent can also improve how it acts, by making its controller execute what it intends more reliably. In this version the controller is fixed, and we leave its online improvement to future work. Benchmarks already evaluate parts of it. Game and embodied benchmarks measure task progress and exploration ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Ouyang et al., 2026 ; Ju et al., 2026 ) . AgentOdyssey measures game progress together with world-knowledge question answering (QA), episodic-memory QA, and exploration coverage in generated text games ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , and ExplorationBench measures how agents acquire knowledge by experimenting in symbolic worlds ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) . Embodied QA asks agents questions about environments they explore ( Das et al., 2018 ; Majumdar et al., 2024 ) . In an embodied world, an agent that improves itself should show it in three ways. It should know more about the world. It should be able to back what it knows with its own experience, pointing to when and where it learned each fact. And it should reach places and information it could not reach before. These are views of the same improvement, but they can come apart, so one of them alone can misread it. A correct answer does not show that the agent observed the evidence for it. In embodied QA, an agent that never reaches a target can still answer 45.5% of questions, about as many as without any images ( Garg et al., 2026 ) . Memory can grow without new discoveries. And progress depends on actions that may not execute as intended. Grounded QA and attribution work show that answers and their supporting evidence need to be checked separately ( Rashkin et al., 2023 ; Xiao et al., 2024 ) . For an agent that explores on its own, the evidence also has to be found in the experience the agent itself collected. Outer Wilds ( Mobius Digital, 2019 ) lets us take the three views on the same run. Its episodes are loops of about 22 minutes. Each loop ends when the Sun goes supernova, the solar system resets, and only the player’s knowledge carries over, as in a multi-episode trial in meta-reinforcement learning ( Duan et al., 2016 ) . What makes it useful is what happens inside each loop. The game defines no explicit task reward. Its progress signals are sparse and implicit, revealing ship-log rumors and reaching places, and most of them are gated by knowledge acquired elsewhere. The knowledge is of different types (Table 2 ). Temporal knowledge decides when a place opens, such as the Ash Twin Project, which can be entered only late in the loop. Spatial and temporal knowledge decide whether the agent can return, since rising sand closes the way out of the Sunless City. Knowledge of function opens routes that look closed, since jellyfish carry the agent through an electrical layer. Knowledge of appearance keeps the agent alive, since ghost matter is invisible to the eye but shows in scout photos. Narrative knowledge explains who built these places, what happened to them, and why, and procedural knowledge, how to reach a place, combines the others. This knowledge comes from evidence of three types (Figure 1 ): observation, what the agent sees, testimony, what it reads or is told, and interaction, what it learns from the results of its own actions ( Gopnik et al., 2004 ; Harris and Koenig, 2006 ) . Collecting that evidence is part of the task. The world changes on its own schedule within each episode, so evidence reachable at one time may be closed later, and actions run through imperfect control. We introduce AstronautBench to measure self-improvement through these three views. After every episode, we measure progress as the cumulative number of places reached and rumor facts revealed, recorded by a game-side mod and never shown to the agent. We measure knowledge with Entity Knowledge QA, 164 single-fact questions written from the game wiki. And we measure grounding by requiring every answer to cite the agent’s own experience: the loop and frame range where it learned the answer, and the body and where it was. In the terms of Tulving (1972) , the answer is semantic knowledge and the citation is episodic memory. An answer counts only when it is correct and attributable to the cited evidence ( Rashkin et al., 2023 ) , as judged by two LLM judges from different model families. We run baselines with GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon planners over eight episodes, acting through a learned controller or their own gamepad inputs. The three views do not move together (Figure 2 ).
