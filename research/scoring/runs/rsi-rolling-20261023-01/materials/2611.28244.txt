arXiv: 2611.28244    title: Demystifying the Autoresearch Harness: How Context and Skills Shape Self-Evolving Agents

[abstract]
Autoresearch agents, which autonomously design, run, and refine experiments, are accelerating scientific progress, particularly in AI research. However, existing agents rely on harnesses that combine multiple mechanisms, making it difficult to determine how individual components contribute to performance. We systematically study harness design for long-horizon, self-evolving autoresearch agents along two dimensions: context management, which determines what an agent retains from its past attempts, and skill management, which governs how it stores and reuses lessons from those attempts. Across MLE-Bench and KernelBench with gpt-oss-120b, gpt-6-luna, and gpt-6-sol, we isolate the effects of individual harness design choices by evaluating each against a recency-truncation baseline within a shared agent framework. For context management, we find that preserving individually accessible records of past attempts is more effective than maintaining a rolling summary, with context folding performing best across all six settings and improving upon the baseline by up to 19.2%. For skill management, selectively discarding persistently unused lessons is more effective on average than consolidating them or promoting them to standing rules. Building on these findings, we combine context folding and skill deletion in MLEvolver, which achieves a 72.7% medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite and a 94% $\mathrm{fast}_1$ score on KernelBench Level 3, substantially outperforming AIDE and OpenEvolve under the same inference budget. Overall, our study distills design principles for preserving experimental history and curating reusable lessons in autoresearch harnesses, paving the way for agents that turn accumulated experience into sustained progress.

Autoresearch agents can automate the cycle at the heart of empirical research: given a task and a grader, they propose an idea, implement it, run the experiment, read the result, and decide what to try next. They are becoming increasingly important in AI for AI (AI4AI), where improving existing AI systems directly accelerates subsequent research. Recent autoresearch agents demonstrate this across diverse domains: on MLE-Bench [ Chan et al., 2025 ] , AIDE [ Jiang et al., 2025 ] , and ML-Master [ Liu et al., 2025b ] iteratively train and tune competitive machine learning pipelines. On KernelBench [ Ouyang et al., 2025 ] , Kevin [ Baronio et al., 2026 ] learns to write and iteratively refine fast CUDA kernels, and AlphaEvolve [ Novikov et al., 2025 ] has even sped up the training of the language models it is built on. What is common among these workflows is their long horizons operation. The agent attempts the same task tens or hundreds of times, receives an evaluation score after each attempt, and iteratively refines its solution. Over such horizons, an agent’s behavior depends not only on its language model, but also on the harness that manages the memory it accumulates about its own past. This memory takes two forms, each under a different kind of pressure. The first is the history of past attempts. It grows with every attempt, and soon exceeds the context window, thereby leading to truncation [ Packer et al., 2023 ] . The second is a library of skills : natural language lessons distilled from earlier attempts and retrieved when a similar situation recurs [ Shinn et al., 2023 , Zhao et al., 2024 ] . The library can grow without limit, but it can become redundant and noisy, which makes the right lessons harder to retrieve. What an agent keeps, condenses, or discards in each form of memory is therefore a central design decision. Many mechanisms have been proposed for managing this memory. Agents page information in and out of context [ Packer et al., 2023 ] , reflect on and summarize past experience [ Park et al., 2023 , Shinn et al., 2023 ] , and extract, revise, and reuse insights or workflows [ Zhao et al., 2024 ] . However, these mechanisms are typically evaluated within multi-component systems where differences in search policies, prompt structure, and tool use confound cross-system comparisons. Even when individual studies ablate their own designs, memory mechanisms are rarely evaluated head-to-head under a shared, unified harness. As a result, it remains unclear what each mechanism contributes on its own. We address these questions through a controlled, bottom-up study of memory harness design in iterative self-improvement , where an autoresearch agent repeatedly attempts the same task and receives empirical feedback after each attempt. This setting strains both memory tiers early: the execution history quickly overflows the context window, while the skill library accumulates redundant lessons. Starting from a minimal recency-truncation baseline, we isolate five memory mechanisms within an otherwise identical agent by changing exactly one component at a time (Figure 2 ). Two manage working context: folding condenses each attempt into an expandable digest, and compaction aggregates older history into a running summary. Three govern the skill library: deletion prunes unused lessons, merging consolidates semantic duplicates, and promotion elevates proven skills into persistent standing rules. We evaluate three model backbones, gpt-oss-120b , gpt-6-luna , and gpt-6-sol , on two AI4AI benchmarks whose feedback differs sharply. On MLE-Bench [ Chan et al., 2025 ] , the agent writes machine learning solutions for Kaggle competitions spanning tabular, text, image, and audio data, and receives a score after each attempt. We report a gold-normalized score (GNS), which is 0 for a chance submission and 100 at the gold medal threshold. On KernelBench [ Ouyang et al., 2025 ] , the agent writes CUDA kernels for PyTorch programs, from single operators to full models, and receives compilation, correctness, and timing feedback. We report the geometric-mean speedup over PyTorch Eager. Within each task, all configurations start from the same seed solution, giving them a common starting point for measuring improvement over iterations. For context management, folding achieves the highest performance among all evaluated mechanisms in all six model–benchmark settings, improving MLE-Bench GNS by up to 7.9% and KernelBench geometric-mean speedup by up to 19.2% over recency truncation. Our context analysis links this advantage to folding’s preservation of a separate digest for every attempt, which exposes more of the agent’s history within the context budget. Compaction compresses older attempts into a single rolling summary, leaving progressively less detail about each experiment as the history grows.
