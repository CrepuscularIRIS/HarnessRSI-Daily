arXiv: 2611.29807    title: Blind to What They Can Fix: A Detection Gap in Self-Improving Agents

[abstract]
AI agents can write programs that carry out a task without calling a model at every step, so the task runs fast and its logic stays open to inspection. Agents improve these programs by running them, studying their failures, and revising the code, a form of recursive self-improvement (RSI). We ask whether this process also yields runtime checks that can be trusted. In five task domains, with up to seven author models, agents bring their programs to high scores. Yet when the environment changes, the checks they were told to write stay silent on half or more of the resulting failures in four domains. These failures can be detected and repaired: a monitor built from records of normal runs catches most of them in three domains, and the same agents usually repair them once called. The checks do not examine the recorded relations that reveal the failures, and the strongest agent writes checks that catch most of them once told that its checks are the only thing that calls it back. An unreported failure stays unrepaired, which limits what self-improvement is worth in autonomous operation. Evaluations of self-improvement should measure not only scores but also whether a system notices its own failures.

[intro/head from cached fulltext]
Programs written by AI agents run tasks efficiently and keep their decision logic in plain view. Such a program makes recurring decisions without calling a large language model before each action. Its logic is written as explicit conditions, rules, and actions that people can read, test, reuse, and change ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . An agent can improve such a program from its own execution: it runs the program, studies where it failed, revises the code, and tests the revision. Each round builds on the last, a feedback-driven form of recursive self-improvement (RSI) ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Cao et al., 2026 ) . Once the program runs on its own, with no model watching each step, a new question arises: will the system notice a new failure and start the next round of improvement? A high task score does not tell us whether a system can notice new failures. During development the agent tests the program and sees every failure. During deployment nobody is watching, so the system itself must decide when something needs attention, and a change in the environment can make a good program fail while every action it takes remains valid. We therefore change regularities of the environment that programs may rely on, confirm that the task can still be done, and measure three things on the same pairs of program and change: task performance, failure detection, and repair when the agent is called. In five task domains, with up to seven author models, agents revise programs to high scores yet do not write checks that reliably detect task failures. Some program formats ask the agent to state what its rules rely on and to check it at every step. These checks stay silent on half or more of the failures in four of the five domains, and no threshold on how often they are contradicted turns them into a usable alarm. The failures themselves are neither hidden nor hard to fix: a general-purpose monitor built from records of normal runs detects most of them in three domains, and the same agents repair most of the missed failures when called again, without being told what changed. The failures that the checks miss are visible in execution records that the checks never look at. We keep the program fixed and add checks on recorded relations, that is, on statistics of the recorded variables. Most misses disappear, even when the 32 added relations are drawn at random. Purpose matters as well. Told that a broken check is the only thing that calls it back, Codex closes most of the gap itself: with new programs its misses fall from 69% to 6% in Seaquest and from 95% to 0% in AppWorld, whereas three open models still miss 84% to 100%. The gap is costly. In replayed deployments a trigger that fires in time recovers most of the lost task value, whereas a perfect repairer behind the program’s own checks recovers almost none (Figure 1 ). We contribute an evaluation framework, evidence of a detection gap, and an analysis of its causes and its consequences for deployment. 1. We introduce a controlled framework for evaluating autonomous maintenance by programmatic agents. It measures task performance, failure detection, and repair on the same pairs of program and change. 2. We identify a detection gap in feedback-driven program improvement. The checks that agents are told to write miss half or more of the task failures in four of five domains, although the failures can be detected from execution records and the same agents repair most of them when they are called. 3. We trace the gap to what the checks cover and measure what it costs. Adding recorded relations to the checks of a fixed program removes most misses. Codex writes checks that miss few failures once it is told what they are for, while open models still miss most. In replayed deployments, timely calls recover most of the lost value, and a better repairer behind the program’s own checks recovers almost none.
