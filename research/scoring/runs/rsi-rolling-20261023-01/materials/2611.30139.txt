arXiv: 2611.30139    title: AutoSECO: Automated Skill Evolution for Cross-Problem Combinatorial Optimization with General-Purpose LLM Agents

[abstract]
General-purpose large language model (LLM) agents can develop complete solvers for combinatorial optimization problems. Their specialization, however, often relies on workflows engineered for individual problems, limiting the reuse of development expertise. We propose AutoSECO, an automated skill evolution method that learns reusable solver-development guidance from experience. The challenge is that external evaluation measures the completed solver, while the skill guides decisions during development. AutoSECO connects this feedback to guidance deficiencies by tracing performance shortfalls through solver code and recorded development decisions. Revisions are expressed as general instructions and retained only if a fresh solver developed under the revised skill achieves a lower mean evaluation gap. Evolved solely on the traveling salesperson problem (TSP), the skill guides fresh agents in developing solvers for capacitated vehicle routing, job-shop scheduling, and three-dimensional bin packing without further skill evolution. On TSP, the final mean benchmark gap is 0.52\%, compared with 0.98\% for the skill-evolution baseline SkillOpt. Across all four problems, the evolved skill achieves better aggregate results than no skill guidance, the initial skill, and SkillOpt. The same frozen skill also supports solver development under alternative model--harness configurations. These findings suggest that solver-development experience can be retained as reusable expertise for general-purpose LLM agents.

[intro/head from cached fulltext]
General-purpose agents built on large language models (LLMs) can reason about goals, use tools, execute code, and revise their actions in response to feedback [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . For combinatorial optimization problems (COPs), these capabilities support both the generation of heuristics or algorithmic components [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] and the development of complete, executable solvers [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] . This development process requires the agent to connect problem analysis and mechanism design with implementation, testing, and revision. Agentic optimization systems often supply expertise through specialized roles, staged workflows, or interfaces to dedicated solvers [ 13 , 14 ] . When expertise is embedded in system design, extending it to a different COP can require additional engineering. This motivates learning development guidance that a general-purpose LLM agent can reuse across heterogeneous COPs. Skills offer a way to express such expertise as editable external guidance. In this work, a skill consists of natural-language instructions that the agent consults during solver development. Its effectiveness depends on how the agent interprets and applies those instructions, so manual design alone does not ensure the intended behavior. Even small wording changes can affect task performance [ 15 ] . A skill should therefore be assessed and revised against the behavior and outcomes it produces. Recent skill evolution methods refine guidance by distilling trajectories, diagnosing failures, or selecting revisions through validation [ 16 , 17 , 18 ] . In optimization, OptSkills learns reusable modeling-and-solving workflows [ 19 ] , and OptiSkill evolves a bank of skills [ 20 ] , both with the correctness of modeling as their primary objective. These studies provide a foundation for retaining experience as guidance, while complete solver development poses a particular challenge in interpreting the available feedback. The difficulty is that feedback for revising a solving skill is indirect. External evaluation scores the completed solver, while the skill guides the decisions through which it is developed. A performance shortfall may reflect a limiting design, an implementation error, or insufficient guidance, and the score alone does not indicate whether or how the skill should change. Revising the guidance therefore requires examining the solver code alongside the observations and decisions recorded during development. This context helps identify where the guidance itself may be missing, ambiguous, or too general to support an effective development decision. We propose Automated Skill Evolution for Combinatorial Optimization (AutoSECO), a method that turns this development evidence into reusable guidance for general-purpose agents. A solver agent develops a complete solver under the current solving skill , while a skill evolver , guided by a fixed evolver skill , examines the resulting evidence. The evolver traces observed weaknesses through solver code and recorded development decisions to identify guidance that may need revision. The changes are expressed as general development instructions and tested by having a fresh agent build a new solver from scratch. A candidate replaces the current skill only if the new solver’s mean evaluation gap is strictly lower than the gap previously recorded under the current skill. Only the solving skill evolves, while the agent configuration, evolver skill, and selection rule remain fixed. Using the traveling salesperson problem (TSP) as the sole source COP, the final AutoSECO skill achieves a mean benchmark gap of 0.52%, compared with 0.98% for SkillOpt. The same skill is held fixed while fresh agents develop solvers for capacitated vehicle routing (CVRP), job-shop scheduling (JSSP), and three-dimensional bin packing (3D-BPP), using each target problem’s task definition and training instances. Across all four COPs, AutoSECO achieves better aggregate results than No Skill, Initial Skill, and SkillOpt. The frozen skill also supports solver development under alternative model–harness configurations. These findings support solver-development experience as a source of reusable COP expertise, expressed through guidance that can be applied across problems and agent configurations.

## III Method
AutoSECO learns reusable guidance for COP solver development by examining the decisions behind an agent’s solver and their implications for solution quality (Fig. 1 ). Solver code and development records provide the context needed to interpret performance shortfalls and identify where the current solving skill may offer insufficient guidance. These insights are expressed as general development instructions, allowing experience with one solver to inform how a general-purpose LLM agent approaches future solver-development tasks. Fresh solver development provides an empirical test of whether the revised guidance helps the agent build a better solver. III-A Solver Development under the Solving Skill The solving skill guides the agent in diagnosing solver limitations and deciding how to address them (Fig. 2 ). The manually written initial skill organizes this process as a ReAct-style development loop, in which the agent uses solver performance and search behavior on training instances to assess and refine its implementation. Within each development run, the skill remains fixed as the agent revises the solver code. Evolution preserves this development loop while refining the guidance for interpreting execution evidence and choosing code revisions. The development evidence concerns both implementation correctness and the use of computational and search capacity. Feasibility and objective checks indicate whether the current solver satisfies the task contract. Performance across training instances, improvement over time, and search traces reveal where it remains weak and how additional computation is used. Continued improvement with low evaluation throughput motivates examining efficiency, while early stagnation despite more computation motivates examining search reach. Here, search reach refers to the moves or solution structures explored within the solver’s runtime budget. The guidance also addresses whether available information informs selection, sharing, and scheduling, for example when search statistics are used to allocate time across search channels. A search channel is a separately budgeted search process. These observations support code revisions within a run and provide the context for subsequent skill revision. Solver implementations are restricted to Python’s standard library, NumPy [ 38 ] , Numba [ 39 ] , and basic SciPy numerical functions [ 40 ] . The agent has no access to web search or external solvers during development. III-B From Evaluation Feedback to Skill Revision For the current skill in round , the skill evolver uses external evaluation results to investigate where the guidance may provide insufficient support for solver development. Guided by the fixed evolver skill, it interprets instance-level results together with the completed solver code and the agent’s development records. These sources answer different questions: the results locate the weakness, the code shows what was implemented, and the record reveals the observations and explanations behind its adoption. The trace links these sources around a development decision that the skill was intended to guide. Patterns in the evaluation results help focus the analysis on weaknesses associated with particular problem scales or structures, recurring across rounds, or concentrated in a tail of high-gap instances. The evolver examines the solver code for design limitations consistent with these patterns and uses the development records to locate the decisions that introduced or retained the relevant design. The observations available to the agent at those points and its recorded explanations provide the context for assessing the corresponding guidance in the current solving skill . This trace provides an explanation consistent with the recorded evidence. It does not isolate the causal effect of an instruction, because a solver reflects interacting development choices. Its purpose is to identify a plausible revision target for empirical assessment. A revision requires evidence that the guidance itself is insufficient, for example because an instruction is missing, ambiguous, conflicting, or too general to inform the decision. A failure to follow clear instructions, an isolated coding error, or an incomparable run does not by itself justify changing the skill. When the evidence does not support a skill-level explanation, the evolver leaves that issue untouched. The resulting revision expresses a general lesson about solver development in terms of problem structure, training observations, and resource limits. It may clarify an instruction, resolve conflicting advice, or introduce a missing diagnostic consideration. The revision is framed around observations and choices available in a future development run. This allows a new agent to apply the lesson using its own task definition and training evidence, without access to the source solver’s benchmark results. For example, low evaluation throughput accompanied by continued improvement on training instances may motivate clearer guidance to check computational efficiency before replacing the search mechanism. The source solver informs the revision, but the guidance does not reproduce its particular algorithm or encode benchmark identities or external evaluation outcomes. The revised skill becomes a candidate , whose value is assessed through fresh solver development in Section III-C . III-C Skill Selection by Fresh Solver Performance A candidate skill is assessed by the quality of the solver a fresh agent develops under its guidance. The agent develops this solver from scratch in a single run, using the same COP task, training instances, and development budget as those used for the retained skill . The candidate skill remains fixed while the agent tests and revises the solver code. A fresh run tests whether the revised guidance remains useful when the agent must make the implementation choices again. The retained solver code and its development record are not carried into this run, so the candidate is assessed through a new development trajectory.
