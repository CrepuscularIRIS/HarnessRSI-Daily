arXiv: 2611.30962    title: Self-Evolving Evaluation Environments for Computer-Use Agents

[abstract]
Computer-use agents (CUAs) are rapidly improving, yet their evaluation remains largely static. Existing benchmarks provide a strong foundation, but fixed difficulty ceilings, limited feature coverage, and coarse aggregate scores progressively weaken their discriminative and diagnostic value. We introduce CUA-SEE, a closed-loop evaluation environment that co-evolves tasks and executable verifiers from rollout evidence. It disentangles three objectives: benchmark evolution expands evaluation scope through cohort-calibrated difficulty and coverage growth; diagnostic evolution increases resolution through agent-conditional capability-boundary probes; and verifier evolution improves precision by translating changing requirements into state-grounded checkpoints with adversarial validation. Across multiple agents, applications, and rounds, CUA-SEE generates challenging, measurable tasks while preserving process-level evidence. Our results position adaptive evaluation as a scalable counterpart to self-evolving agents, continuously tracking what agents can do, where their boundaries lie, and how reliably progress is measured.

Agent evaluation, which underpins both scientific progress and real-world deployment, has long been a fundamentally yet challenging problem [ 6 , 21 , 14 , 11 ] . For computer-use agents (CUAs) [ 8 , 10 , 19 , 23 ] , evaluation is harder because success must be verified against intricate software-specific states and multifaceted task requirements, rather than textual responses (e.g., search agents [ 9 , 7 , 2 ] ) or binary execution results (e.g., code agents [ 5 ] ). Current benchmarks [ 18 , 20 ] have established an essential foundation for CUA evaluation by manually curating realistic tasks and task-specific verifiers. However, this human-authored, one-shot construction paradigm is costly to scale and renders both tasks and verifiers largely fixed as agent capabilities evolve, giving rise to several critical limitations [ 17 , 4 , 22 ] . This static paradigm creates three consequential limitations: (1) A fixed task set imposes a difficulty ceiling : once agents master its tasks, scores saturate and cease to distinguish further progress. (2) Manual construction provides limited feature coverage ; in our audit of core OSWorld applications, the existing tasks exercise less than 20% of the available software-feature taxonomy. (3) Static benchmarks lack evaluation dynamics : they neither track a moving capability boundary nor actively search for newly emerging weaknesses, shortcut behaviors, and generalization gaps. These limitations reveal the necessity of an evaluation system that evolves in tandem with the LLM agents it assesses. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose CUA-SEE , a S elf- E volving E valuation environment for computer-use agents. CUA-SEE reconceptualizes evaluation as a dynamic, closed-loop process where tasks, executable verifiers, and agent rollout evidence continuously inform and refine one another. At each round, agents interact with the current task-verifier pairs; validity-filtered trajectories and checkpoint outcomes update the measurement state; and this evidence determines what tasks should be measured in the next round and how the corresponding verifiers should be implemented. Specifically, our framework is designed around three core components that work in concert to disentangle three dimensions often conflated in conventional task-hardening pipelines: evaluation scope, diagnostic resolution, and measurement precision . Scope. To address the fixed difficulty ceiling and feature coverage, we expand the evaluation scope of static benchmarks by introducing an evolution operator, called Benchmark Task Evolution , which constructs a shared, model-neutral benchmark from aligned trajectories of a heterogeneous agent cohort. It retains all replicates, separates execution validity from task success, and uses cohort agreement and disagreement to calibrate whether a source task is mastered, discriminating, unstable, or confounded. Evolution then optimizes two suite-level objectives: advancing the measurable difficulty frontier and expanding coverage over exact software-feature-operation cells and capability-intensity cells. Candidate descendants may increase precision, scale, state dependence, procedure depth, feature composition, or add a previously uncovered but coherent operation. Each descendant preserves a semantic lineage anchor and must introduce explicit, independently observable measurement obligations, while a round-level registry coordinates coverage growth across tasks. Benchmark evolution thus broadens what the suite can measure without tailoring the benchmark to an idiosyncratic failure of one agent. Resolution. The second operator, called Diagnostic Task Evolution , is agent-conditional and concentrates evaluation budget near uncertain capability boundaries. A batch-level planner aggregates longitudinal evidence into capability, software, and feature maps to dispatch targeted experiments over seven calibrated dimensions such as precision control and state-dependent reasoning, as detailed in Appendix A . The task-level evolver realizes each experiment as a natural task with one primary dimension at a controlled intensity. The search is adaptive rather than monotonically harder: Clean successes and failures prompt upward and downward probes, respectively; partial or censored runs trigger deconfounding. This adaptive bracketing refines where an agent’s pass-fail transition lies, turning coarse benchmark scores into locally attributable capability evidence. Precision. Both task operators share a requirement-preserving measurement backbone, termed as Verifier Evolution . The third component uses task-agnostic software adapters to probe native state-export surfaces and normalize them into a shared CanonicalState schema.
