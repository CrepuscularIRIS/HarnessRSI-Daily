arXiv: 2611.33909    title: EGSE: Evidence-Grounded Skill Evolution for Medical Reasoning Agents

[abstract]
Medical reasoning agents combine foundation models with specialized tools to interpret medical images, yet reusing their historical experience remains challenging. A correct final answer can conceal flawed reasoning or tool use, synthesized advice may lack source support, and superficially similar cases may require different diagnostic procedures. In this paper, we propose EGSE, an evidence-grounded skill evolution framework that distills historical dialogues from similar cases into reusable conditional workflows and progressively refines a multi-faceted skill library through supported and validated updates to improve medical agents. EGSE evaluates intermediate reasoning and tool use and contrasts repeated successful and failed rollouts to extract useful practices and evidence-supported avoidance guidance. These experiences are synthesized into workflows with applicability conditions and checks traceable to source dialogues. Library additions and merges require source-grounded review and execution validation. At inference time, EGSE routes queries to skill nodes organized by imaging modality, question task type, and disease category, then retrieves and selects applicable workflows based on their conditions, the current images, and the question to guide evidence acquisition and interpretation. Experiments across four chest X-ray benchmarks show that EGSE outperforms representative medical agents and skill evolution methods in reasoning accuracy while reducing tool calls. It also delivers consistent accuracy gains across seven foundation models and supports skill transfer across datasets.

Medical image interpretation requires integrating visual findings with clinical context and selecting appropriate procedures to distinguish competing explanations. Building on medical foundation models [ 18 , 25 , 6 , 2 ] , medical agents [ 9 , 23 ] integrate LLM [ 1 , 11 ] reasoning with specialized neural tools [ 8 , 20 ] to acquire visual evidence through classification, detection, segmentation, and visual question answering. For example, MedRAX [ 9 ] integrates chest X-ray analysis tools into interactive reasoning, while CXRAgent [ 23 ] combines tool-output validation with diagnostic planning and expert collaboration. These advances support evidence acquisition within individual cases [ 17 , 48 , 36 ] . Across cases posing similar clinical questions, however, the logic of what evidence to acquire and how to interpret it can be reused [ 32 , 26 , 47 ] . Extracting this logic from historical rollouts and invoking it appropriately in new cases remain challenging. Recent medical-agent research explores experience-driven self-evolution through memory-based reflection in AMC [ 16 ] , composite-tool discovery in MACRO [ 10 ] , template and tool evolution in STELLA [ 13 ] , experience-guided planning in HealthFlow [ 49 ] , and skill-memory evolution in SkeMeX [ 32 ] . Across these approaches, historical interactions provide feedback for extracting reusable procedural knowledge, which is stored as memories, tools, plans, or skills and retrieved or invoked to guide subsequent tasks. Turning this experience into reliable workflows raises three questions. First, are the extracted practices and accepted updates useful? Final outcomes alone do not identify useful intermediate practices, extracted advice can remain specific to its source case, and an update can impair an existing workflow. Second, is the synthesized workflow supported? Even useful-looking content can introduce advice absent from the source dialogues. Third, is a retrieved workflow applicable to the current case? Superficially similar cases may differ in anatomy, visual findings, or question requirements. Figure 1 illustrates the opportunity: historical cases share a procedure for identifying relevant cues, examining discriminative imaging features, and evaluating competing explanations. Applied to a new case, this procedure guides the agent to verify anatomical location and internal contents before resolving competing diagnoses. This motivates our hypothesis that useful, source-supported practices can be distilled into conditional workflows and reused when their conditions match new cases. To realize this hypothesis, we propose EGSE, an evidence-grounded skill evolution framework that progressively refines an external multi-faceted skill library for medical image interpretation. Each skill node is indexed by imaging modality, question task type, and disease category and contains multiple conditional workflows. To identify useful practices, EGSE scores intermediate reasoning and tool use against ground-truth answers, extracts candidate recommendations from positively scored content shared by successful rollouts and avoidance advice from negatively scored content shared by failed rollouts, and uses semantic subtraction to retain recommendations that do not conflict with the avoidance advice under overlapping applicability conditions. These operations aim to retain reusable workflow steps, termed checks to distinguish them from dialogue steps, rather than case-specific conclusions. Execution validation tests proposed changes on the current case and a previous case belonging to the same skill node to screen for harmful updates. To ground synthesis in source evidence, each workflow check cites atomic experiences linked to source dialogue-step pairs, and source-based review assesses these support links. To assess applicability, multi-faceted routing identifies a relevant node, while condition-based retrieval and image-aware selection choose among its workflows. Only changes that pass review and execution validation enter the library; both model parameters and the library remain frozen at test time. We evaluate EGSE on four chest X-ray benchmarks, including ChestAgentBench [ 9 ] , CheXbench [ 6 ] , MIMIC-CXR [ 14 ] , and SLAKE-VQA [ 21 ] . On ChestAgentBench, with Qwen3.7-Flash [ 28 ] as the foundation model, EGSE improves accuracy from 73.75% to 77.70% over MedRAX while reducing average tool calls per case from 5.8 to 2.3. It also outperforms the general-domain skill-evolution methods Trace2Skill [ 26 ] and MACRO, as well as the medical-domain method SkeMeX. Across seven foundation models, EGSE consistently improves accuracy on ChestAgentBench, with gains ranging from 2.58% to 5.84%. We further evaluate cross-dataset skill transfer and within-dataset skill construction on external datasets.
