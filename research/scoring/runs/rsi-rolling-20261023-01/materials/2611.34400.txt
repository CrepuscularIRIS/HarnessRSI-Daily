arXiv: 2611.34400    title: EvoLink-Bench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Self-Evolving Agents in Long-Horizon Interdependent Task Streams

[abstract]
Large language model (LLM) agents are evolving from passive chatbots into autonomous systems that operate continuously in dynamic environments. Such agents need not only solve current tasks but also learn from past interactions to improve future performance. However, existing streaming benchmarks often rely on short task sequences or largely independent tasks, providing limited evidence of whether agents can effectively reuse experience when later tasks depend on earlier outcomes. To address this gap, we introduce \textbf{EvoLink-Bench}, a benchmark for evaluating self-evolving agents in long-horizon, interdependent task streams. It covers daily office work, multi-turn dialogue, and AutoResearch-style workflows, with persistent workspaces and dependencies across tasks. The benchmark includes two complementary levels: \textbf{L1}, which evaluates the acquisition and revision of language-expressible knowledge, and \textbf{L2}, which assesses the development of task-level capabilities through execution and feedback. Its evaluation further distinguishes within-task recovery, persistent-state updates, and experience reuse in later tasks. We evaluate five agent frameworks across three backbone configurations under three persistence conditions, covering 19,635 scored task slots from selected evaluation cohorts. Results show that no framework consistently performs best across both levels, retries recover only a limited proportion of failed tasks, and persistent-state updates do not necessarily improve subsequent performance. Furthermore, the effects of evolution differ between knowledge- and capability-level tasks, suggesting that these two forms of evolution require different learning mechanisms. Performance gains also vary across domains and task streams, with improvements on some tasks not necessarily extending to others.

Large language model (LLM) agents are rapidly evolving from stateless problem solvers into interactive, lifelong assistants ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Xi et al., 2023 ) . In real-world deployments, agents are expected to do more than answer isolated queries; they need to assist users continuously over extended periods. During this process, interactions can generate valuable experience, such as user preferences revealed through dialogue, corrections to tool usage, or newly created task artifacts. This calls for self-evolving agents that can not only complete immediate tasks but also learn from past interactions to improve future decision-making ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Majumder et al., 2024 ) . The ability to learn continuously during service is therefore a key requirement for practical self-evolving agents. Such continuous deployment creates a complex and highly interconnected environment. Unlike a sequence of independent tasks, the user’s history, the external environment, and the agent’s workspace may all change over time. For example, an initial data analysis task may produce a table that serves as the basis for a subsequent strategic decision, while an automated research workflow may generate code and checkpoints that inform a later experimental hypothesis ( Lu et al., 2024 ) . Moreover, later tasks may challenge earlier assumptions, require the agent to revisit a capability after a long interval, or introduce unrelated tasks that compete for its attention. To operate effectively in such environments, agents must not only retain and reuse relevant experience but also revise outdated knowledge and manage interference, while maintaining the consistency of the persistent workspace on which future tasks depend. As research on self-evolving agents continues to advance, evaluating their capabilities in realistic continual-service settings has become increasingly important. Existing benchmarks cover individual agent capabilities and application-specific settings ( Yehudai et al., 2026 ) , but address only parts of this broader problem. Short streaming benchmarks typically use repeated tasks to evaluate local transfer and reflection ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) . Longer task streams can reveal experience accumulation and cross-domain interference, but are often constructed by concatenating independently specified tasks, without requiring later tasks to depend on earlier outputs or decisions ( Wei et al., 2025 ; Yan et al., 2026 ) . Meanwhile, some stateful interactive benchmarks preserve tools and world states, but focus on narrower scenarios with task-specific protocols ( Trivedi et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Cai et al., 2025 ; Fu et al., 2026 ) . As a result, existing benchmarks provide limited insight into whether agents can continuously improve through experience across diverse, long-running interactions, particularly when later tasks depend on earlier decisions and artifacts. To address this gap, we introduce EvoLink-Bench , a benchmark designed to evaluate self-evolving agents in long-horizon, interdependent task streams. Figure 1 illustrates its overall design, including two evolution levels, a persistent task environment, and a unified evaluation protocol. Unlike benchmarks that evaluate isolated tasks or simply concatenate independent examples, EvoLink-Bench organizes tasks into persistent streams, where user context and workspace artifacts carry across interactions. It covers daily office work, multi-turn dialogue, software and data tasks, and automated research workflows. More importantly, tasks are connected through explicit dependencies: later tasks may require agents to recall earlier facts and decisions or reuse previously generated code, data, and experimental results. By varying the intervals between related tasks and introducing intervening tasks, the benchmark examines whether agents can reuse and transfer experience, revise outdated knowledge, and handle interference over time. EvoLink-Bench evaluates self-evolution at two complementary levels. L1 focuses on knowledge evolution, examining whether agents can acquire, retain, apply, and revise information that can be expressed in language, such as facts, preferences, and procedural rules. L2 focuses on capability evolution, examining whether agents can develop reusable task-solving procedures through execution and feedback, including coding, data analysis, and research workflows. This distinction captures two different aspects of learning: knowing what to remember and improving how to perform tasks. To evaluate these processes, we introduce three persistence conditions: Reset, Frozen, and Evolving, which differ in whether previous state is retained and whether post-turn updates are enabled. With bounded attempts, our evaluation further separates within-task recovery from changes to persistent agent state and their potential benefits on later probe tasks.
